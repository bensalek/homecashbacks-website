What Is Multiple Representation?

When the same brokerage or agent represents both sides of a deal, something real changes: your agent legally can't advise you on price anymore. Here's what that means, and how to avoid it entirely.

The short answer

Both clients lose full representation, not just one

Multiple representation happens when the same brokerage, or under designated representation the same individual agent, represents both the buyer and the seller in one transaction, or represents more than one competing buyer for the same property. Under Ontario's Trust in Real Estate Services Act (TRESA), this is legal, but only with full written disclosure and the consent of every client involved, and it changes what your agent is actually allowed to do for you.

What changes in multiple representation
The agent can't advise on the price to offer or accept
No advice on terms that could favour one side
Consent is required from every client, and is optional
The agent becomes a neutral facilitator, not an advocate
The real cost, in plain terms

What you actually lose

This is the part most people don't realize until it's explained directly. Once multiple representation is in effect, the agent representing you can't tell you whether to offer more, whether to hold firm, or whether a counter-offer is a fair one. They can't share what they might otherwise know about the other side's motivation. Effectively, you're negotiating on your own, just with someone filling out the paperwork.

This isn't a technicality, it's the actual regulatory standard
Per RECO's own guidance to agents: in multiple representation, none of the clients are fully represented. The clients no longer receive the full benefit of the client relationship, because of restrictions on the services that can be provided, the information that can be shared, and the advice that can be given.
Two different models

Brokerage representation vs. designated representation

Brokerage representationDesignated representation
Who owes you the dutyEvery agent at the brokerageOnly your specific named agent
When multiple representation arisesAny time the brokerage has both a buyer and seller client in the same deal, regardless of which agentsOnly if the same individual agent represents both sides
Confidential informationEffectively shared across the brokerageProtected from other agents at the same brokerage

Neither model is inherently better, but the distinction matters for understanding how easily multiple representation can arise with a given brokerage. A large brokerage using brokerage representation can end up in multiple representation simply because two different agents at the same firm each have a client interested in the same property, even if those agents have never spoken to each other.

Your options

You never have to consent

Consent is required, in writing, from every client involved If you don't consent, the brokerage cannot proceed with multiple representation.
A different designated representative may be an option Some brokerages can assign a different agent within the same firm to represent you fully instead.
You can seek representation elsewhere Nothing obligates you to stay with a brokerage that can't offer you full, unconflicted representation.
