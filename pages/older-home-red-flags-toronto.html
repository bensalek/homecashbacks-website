Older Home Red Flags Toronto

Toronto has a lot of pre-1980 housing stock. Here's what to check for, why insurers care so much, and what it actually costs to fix in 2026.

The short answer

Five systems worth checking by era

None of these automatically disqualify a home, but they can significantly affect your ability to get insurance, and insurance is often required before a lender will fund your mortgage. Knowing the approximate era a system was common lets you ask the right question before you fall in love with a listing.

The one that surprises buyers most

Knob and tube wiring can make a home genuinely hard to insure

This is worth taking seriously in 2026. Most major insurers now decline coverage outright on any property where knob and tube circuits remain energized, not just charge more for it. If a home still has active knob and tube, the realistic path to insurability is a full rewire by a Licensed Electrical Contractor, followed by an ESA (Electrical Safety Authority) inspection and Certificate of Inspection, the document insurers actually require before they'll reinstate coverage.

The complication buyers don't see coming
Many older Toronto homes with knob and tube also have vermiculite attic insulation, often sold under the Zonolite brand, which was frequently contaminated with asbestos until around 1990. If both are present, the electrician generally can't fish new wires through the attic until the vermiculite is professionally abated first, adding real cost and time to a rewire that's already necessary for insurance.
The 1960s-70s issue

Aluminum wiring, and how to spot it

Aluminum wiring is legal under the Ontario Electrical Safety Code as long as proper aluminum-to-copper connectors were used, but many insurers still charge meaningfully higher premiums or decline coverage entirely. A licensed electrician can inspect and, if needed, install the proper connectors or recommend a partial or full rewire.

Check the plastic casing on visible wiringIt's often printed with "Aluminum" or "Alum" where accessible, such as in an unfinished basement.
Look for silver-coloured wire at outletsCopper is reddish-brown; aluminum has a distinct silver tone.
Watch for hot or discoloured outletsA sign of a poor connection, worth a professional look regardless of wiring type.
Insulation and pipes

UFFI, asbestos, and galvanized pipes

UFFI (Urea Formaldehyde Foam Insulation) was used from 1975 to 1979 and banned in 1980. Off-gassing decreases significantly over time, and its presence must be disclosed on the listing and in the agreement.

Asbestos was common in pipe wrapping, floor tiles, and insulation until the 1980s. There's no Ontario law banning the sale of a home containing it. The real distinction is intact versus friable (crumbled): friable asbestos can release airborne fibres and is genuinely hazardous, while intact, undisturbed asbestos is a lower-urgency issue that a specialist can advise on.

Galvanized pipes, common until the 1950s, corrode from the inside out over time, leading to reduced water pressure and a real risk of leaks. A plumber can confirm the condition and remaining service life.
