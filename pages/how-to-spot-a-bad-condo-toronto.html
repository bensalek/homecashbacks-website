How to Spot a Bad Condo

Real GTA condo owners have faced $40,000+ special assessments with two weeks' notice. Here's how to see it coming before you sign, and a legal protection most buyers don't know exists.

Why this matters more than people think

This isn't a rare, freak occurrence

Special assessment stories out of the GTA aren't hypothetical. Owners in real Toronto and GTA buildings have faced assessments ranging from a few thousand dollars to well over $40,000 per unit, sometimes with payment deadlines as short as two weeks, sometimes tied to a lien on the unit if they can't pay. This isn't about scaring you off condos, most are managed well. It's about knowing which signals to check before you're the one getting that letter.

A protection most buyers don't know exists

The 10% rule

If fees jump more than 10% in a year, you may be able to walk away
If a condo board raises maintenance fees by more than 10% in a single year, that's generally treated as a material change. If this happens during your condition period, it can give you legal grounds to walk away from the purchase entirely and get your deposit back. Most buyers have never heard of this, and it's worth telling your lawyer to specifically watch for it during their status certificate review.
The patterns that actually show up

Real warning signs, not vague ones

!
Maintenance fees that seem too low for the building's age and size Sometimes it's genuinely efficient management. Other times, the corporation is covering costs through regular special assessments instead of the monthly fee, which won't show up as a red flag on the fee line alone.
!
A big jump after years of modest increases A building with 3% annual increases suddenly jumping to 15-20% in one year is a strong signal something was underfunded or deferred for a long time.
!
A reserve fund significantly below what the reserve fund study recommends This is the single clearest number to check. A large gap between actual and recommended balance means a special assessment is a matter of when, not if.
!
A recently paid-off special assessment A seller can pay off an outstanding assessment before listing so it doesn't appear as an active line item, even though it still reflects a building that needed one. Ask directly whether any assessments have been levied in the past several years, not just whether one is currently outstanding.
!
A building older than 10-15 years with no major system upgrades on record Roofs, elevators, windows, and parking garage membranes all have a finite lifespan. A building that's never touched them is closer to needing to than one that has.

What to actually do with this

Reading the status certificate for these signals

Compare the actual reserve fund balance to the study's recommended balanceNot just whether a reserve fund exists, whether it's actually adequate.
Ask for the last 3-5 years of fee history, not just the current amountA single snapshot tells you almost nothing. A trend tells you a lot.
Ask about assessments levied in the past several years, not just currently outstanding onesA paid-off assessment still tells you something real about the building.
Have your lawyer review the full status certificate, not just the summaryThe details that matter most are often in the financial statements, not the cover letter.
