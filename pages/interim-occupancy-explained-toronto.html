Interim Occupancy Explained

You can move in before you own it. Here's what that gap actually costs, and why none of it builds equity.

The short answer

Occupancy and ownership are two different dates

When you buy a pre-construction condo, you may be able to move into your completed unit before the rest of the building finishes construction. This period is called interim occupancy. You can occupy the unit, but you don't yet own it, title only transfers once the condominium is officially registered with the municipality. Until then, you pay the builder a monthly occupancy fee instead of a mortgage.

How long you should expect to wait

Commonly 6-12 months, sometimes much longer

Interim occupancy can last anywhere from a few weeks to two years or more, depending on the building's size and how quickly registration is completed. Larger developments generally take longer, and lower-floor units are often ready before higher floors, meaning your specific unit's timeline can differ from the building's overall schedule.

What most buyers get wrong

This money doesn't build equity

The occupancy fee is closer to rent than a mortgage payment
None of your occupancy fee reduces the purchase price or builds you any equity, since you don't legally own the unit yet. It's sometimes called "phantom rent" for exactly this reason. Your mortgage only begins at final closing, once the building is registered and title transfers to you.

The fee is made up of three parts: interest on the unpaid balance of your purchase price, calculated using the Bank of Canada's prescribed one-year conventional mortgage rate, an estimated contribution toward the building's common expenses, and estimated property taxes. A larger deposit reduces the interest portion, since it's calculated on what you still owe.

The interest component moves with the Bank of Canada's prescribed rate at the time, so ask your builder for a current estimate rather than relying on a figure from when you first signed.

Before you take occupancy

What to have ready

Post-dated cheques for the occupancy periodBuilders commonly ask for these upfront, often 12 at a time.
Proof of unit insuranceRequired before you receive keys, even though you don't own the unit yet.
Utilities set up in your nameHydro is almost always separately metered, even during interim occupancy.
Confirmed your mortgage is ready to fund at final closingGiven market shifts since your original agreement, start this early, values and appraisals can change over a long interim period.

If you're planning to rent your unit out during interim occupancy, this needs to be negotiated into your Agreement of Purchase and Sale at the time of purchase, or approved separately by the builder. It's not automatically allowed under most standard agreements.
