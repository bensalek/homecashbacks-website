Home Inspection Toronto

A few hundred dollars to know what you're actually buying. Here's what it costs, what it covers, and when waiving it is a reasonable risk versus a costly one.

The short answer

A visual check of the home's major systems

A home inspection is a professional, non-invasive review of a property's condition, arranged after your offer is accepted and before the deal closes. The inspector examines the structure and main systems, and notes what works, what needs repair, and what might fail soon. It is not legally required in Ontario, it's a standard offer condition you choose to include, or waive, for your own protection.

Standard inspection vs. specialized testing

Included in a standard inspectionExtra, if requested
Roof condition and visible drainageRadon testing ($200–$500)
Foundation and structural elementsMould or air quality testing ($200–$500)
Electrical panel and visible wiring signsAsbestos testing
Plumbing leaks and pressure signsWETT inspection for wood-burning appliances
Heating and cooling system age and conditionSewer scope

Inspections are visual and non-invasive, the inspector doesn't move furniture, open up walls, or test water quality unless you've specifically arranged for it. If a home has a wood-burning fireplace or stove, or you have specific concerns about mould or radon, ask about adding these separately.

The real decision

When waiving the inspection is a reasonable risk

Waiving inspection can strengthen your offer in a competitive bidding situation, but you accept the home's condition as-is, with essentially no recourse for issues found after closing unless you can prove the seller deliberately concealed them, which is difficult to do. It's a real trade-off, not a formality.

When it's a more reasonable risk to waive
A newer home (under roughly 10 to 15 years old), a unit in a well-managed condo building where major systems are the corporation's responsibility, or a situation where the seller has already provided a recent pre-listing inspection report you can review. Outside of those, waiving is a bigger gamble, particularly on older detached homes where structural or system issues are far more common.
