Home Pricing Strategy Toronto

Underprice to spark a bidding war, or price at market value? Both are real strategies. Here's when each one actually works.

The short answer

Two real strategies, not one right answer

Toronto sellers generally choose between two approaches. Neither is universally correct, the right one depends on your home, your neighbourhood, and current buyer demand, not a formula that applies to every listing.

Strategy one

Underpricing to create competition

List meaningfully below expected value, often $200,000 to $400,000 under on higher-priced Toronto properties, to draw a large pool of buyers and a scheduled offer date. When it works, multiple offers push the price above what market-value pricing alone would have achieved.

When it tends to work When it tends to backfire
Strong, proven demand for the specific home type and neighbourhood Slower market or less in-demand property type
Well-presented, move-in ready condition Needs visible work or updates
Comparable recent sales support a bidding war outcome Thin or inconsistent comparable sales data

The real risk: if a bidding war doesn't materialize, you can end up with a single offer below true market value, or no offers at all on the scheduled date, forcing a relist at a higher price that can make buyers wonder what's wrong with the property.

Strategy two

Pricing at market value

Price at what comparable sales actually suggest the home is worth, and tell buyers exactly what you expect. It's more predictable and avoids the emotional volatility of an offer-date gamble, though it typically won't generate the same bidding-war upside when conditions are genuinely in a seller's favour.

Why some sellers prefer it
More predictable outcome, less emotional stress
Buyers negotiate with real numbers, not guesses
Well respected by buyers who avoid bidding wars
Lower risk of an underwhelming offer date
The one universal rule

Overpricing is the real mistake, not the strategy you pick

Whichever strategy you choose, overpricing is what actually costs sellers money. An overpriced home sits longer, gathers fewer showings, and buyers start assuming something is wrong with it. It very often ends up selling for less than accurate initial pricing would have achieved. If a listing gets little activity in the first two weeks, that's a signal to reassess, not a reason to wait longer.
