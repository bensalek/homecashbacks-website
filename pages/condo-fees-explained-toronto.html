Condo Fees Explained

What that monthly number actually pays for, how it's calculated, and real per-square-foot benchmarks so you know what's normal.

The short answer

A bundle of costs a freehold owner pays separately

If you owned a freehold home, you'd pay separately for building insurance, exterior maintenance, landscaping, snow removal, and your own savings toward major repairs like a roof or furnace. In a condo, all of this is bundled into one monthly fee, officially called common expenses.

What a typical fee covers
Building insurance (structure and common areas)
Common area cleaning, landscaping, snow removal
Security, concierge, and amenity upkeep
Mandatory reserve fund contribution

The building's own insurance only covers the structure and common areas, not your personal contents or unit improvements, which is why you still need your own condo unit owner's insurance policy separately.

How the number is set

Fees are based on your ownership share, not a flat rate

Your fee is calculated from your unit's proportionate share of ownership in the building, generally tied to square footage, as set out in the condominium's declaration. A larger unit pays a larger share of the total annual operating budget. Fees typically increase once a year when the board reviews and approves the budget.

Real benchmarks

What's normal, by building age

Building ageTypical fee per sq ft
Newer (0-5 years)$0.65 to $0.75
Mid-age (5-15 years)$0.75 to $0.85
Older (15+ years)$0.85 to $0.95+

Buildings with extensive amenities, pools, 24-hour concierge, multiple elevators, tend to run higher regardless of age. A 700 sq ft unit at $0.80/sq ft works out to roughly $560 a month, a useful sanity check when comparing listings.

The real reason your fee matters

The reserve fund is the part that actually protects you

Every condo corporation is legally required to contribute a portion of fees to the reserve fund, the building's long-term savings account for major future repairs like roof replacements, garage resurfacing, and elevator overhauls. Ontario corporations must commission a reserve fund study on a regular cycle, so the contribution level is based on an engineering projection of what the building will actually need, not a guess.

A low fee isn't automatically a good deal
A suspiciously low fee can mean the corporation is underfunding its reserve or deferring maintenance, setting owners up for a special assessment later. Judge a fee by what it covers and the building's financial health, not by the number alone. See our full breakdown of financial red flags to check for.
