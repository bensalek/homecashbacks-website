Understand the structure

How a Condo Is Different

With a freehold home, you own the building and the land. With a condo, you own your unit and a share of everything else. That everything else is managed by a condo corporation to run by a board elected by unit owners.

The corporation sets your monthly fee, manages the reserve fund, and can charge you for unexpected costs. Its financial health directly affects yours.

You own
Your unit interior
Your appliances and finishes
Your parking spot and locker
Corporation manages
Lobby, hallways, amenities
Roof, elevators, mechanical systems
Reserve fund for major repairs
The key risk: If the corporation is poorly managed or underfunded, you can be hit with a special assessment to a one-time charge that can run tens of thousands of dollars. This is why you don't just buy a unit. You buy into a building.

The Status Certificate

This document reveals the building's financial and legal health. Your lawyer reviews it before you firm up.

Three questions your lawyer must answer:

01
Is the reserve fund at least 70% funded?
Below that, a special assessment is likely. Factor it into your offer price or walk away.
02
Is there active litigation against the corporation?
Can block your mortgage approval with some lenders. Talk to your lawyer before proceeding.
03
Is there a pending special assessment?
Already approved but not yet collected means you pay it after closing. Quantify it first.

Once you have the keys

After You Close

Closing day is not the finish line. A few things need to happen in the first weeks that buyers commonly forget.

Get condo unit owner's insurance
The condo corporation's policy covers the building, not your contents or interior finishes. Have this in place before closing, most lenders require proof.
Set up utilities in your name
Hydro is almost always separately metered. Confirm with property management whether water and heat are included in your condo fees or need to be set up separately.
Register with property management
Get your fob or key, confirm parking and locker assignment in writing, and book the elevator for move-in if the building requires it.
Update your address
Driver's licence, health card, mail forwarding, and your bank. Easy to forget in the chaos of moving.
Read the condo corporation's rules
Every building has its own rules on noise, renovations, guest parking, and short-term rentals. Know them before you accidentally break one.

Questions to Ask When Buying a Condo

Ask these before you fall in love with a unit. The listing agent may not volunteer the answers.

What is the reserve fund balance, and when was the last study done?
A healthy reserve fund lowers your risk of a surprise special assessment down the line.
Are there any planned or pending special assessments?
This should be in the status certificate, but ask directly too. Get it in writing.
Is the building involved in any litigation?
Active lawsuits against the condo corporation can affect your ability to get financing.
What percentage of units are rented rather than owner-occupied?
A high rental ratio can affect building upkeep and, in some cases, your mortgage approval.
Have condo fees increased in each of the last few years, and by how much?
Steady, modest increases are normal. Large jumps year after year are a warning sign.
What exactly is included in the fee, and what is not?
Heat, water, and amenities are sometimes included, sometimes not. Get the specific breakdown in writing.
Are pets, rentals, and short-term rentals allowed?
Rules vary significantly by building and can affect your plans if you intend to rent the unit out later.
Is parking and a locker included, or sold separately?
Confirm this is specified in the listing and, if included, confirmed in writing before you firm up your offer.

Red Flags to Watch For

None of these are automatic deal-breakers, but each one deserves a real explanation before you go firm.

An underfunded reserve fund
If the reserve fund study shows the fund is significantly below recommended levels, a special assessment may be coming.
Watch out
Active or repeated litigation against the corporation
Ongoing lawsuits can complicate financing and signal deeper governance issues in the building.
Watch out
Large, unexplained fee increases
A jump well above inflation, without a clear reason, is worth a direct question to the property manager.
Watch out
Very high rental unit ratio
Some lenders limit financing above certain rental ratios, and it can affect building upkeep over time.
Watch out
Visible deferred maintenance
Worn common areas, broken fixtures, or an unkempt lobby often reflect how the corporation is actually managed day to day.
Watch out
A status certificate that arrives incomplete or late
Corporations required to move quickly usually do. Delays or missing pages are worth asking about directly.
